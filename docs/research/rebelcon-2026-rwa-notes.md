**Slide: how ownership records evolved**

1. **1602 — Paper certificates.** Ownership on paper; investors buy a share of a company (Dutch East India Company's tradable shares).
2. **1973 — Electronic records.** Ownership in centralized databases; institutions transfer securities electronically (DTC book-entry).
3. **Today — Tokenized liquid assets.** Ownership on blockchains: dollars, Treasuries and equities issued and traded on-chain, via wrappers and native issuance.
4. **Future — Tokenized illiquid assets.** Ownership of things that never traded: a share of a building, IP, land or infrastructure, with on-chain title and servicing.

Takeaway: blockchains expand *what can be made investable*, not just how it settles. Note the tension with today's data — illiquid assets like real estate are still under 1% of the market, so this is his bet on where it goes, not where it is.

## Plain-English basics

**Blockchain** — a shared, permanent record of who owns what, kept by thousands of computers instead of one company. Think of a bar tab everyone in the bar can see and nobody can erase. Transactions are grouped into "blocks" (pages), checked by the network, then locked and chained to the page before. Changing an old page would break every page after it, so it's very hard to cheat. It never closes — 24/7.

**Smart contract** — a small program on the blockchain that runs automatically when conditions are met, like a vending machine: put in $2, press B4, get chips — no cashier. Examples: paying daily interest to token holders, blocking transfers to unapproved wallets, swapping a tokenized stock for dollars instantly, splitting rent among thousands of owners. Catch: code does exactly what it says, bugs included, and there's often no undo.

**Tokenizing** — writing ownership of a real asset (a T-bill, a stock, a building) into a blockchain instead of a bank's private database.

**Ethereum vs Solana** — two different blockchains. Ethereum: older, most trusted, where Wall Street money sits. Solana: faster and cheaper, where retail users and 24/7 stock trading are growing.

## What RWAs are

RWAs (real-world assets) are traditional assets — Treasury bills, loans, gold, stocks, real estate — whose ownership is recorded as a token on a blockchain. The asset itself stays off-chain; the token is the receipt that proves you own a piece of it.

Think of it like a coat-check ticket: the coat (the asset) sits in a back room with a custodian, and the ticket (the token) can be handed around, traded or used as collateral 24/7. The ticket is only worth something if the coat is really there and the coat-check is honest.

The SEC's position since January 2026: a security issued as a token is still the same security, under the same laws.

## How tokenization works

Every RWA product is a chain of trust from a real asset to a token in a wallet.

1. **Asset** — a real thing exists: T-bills, a loan book, gold bars, shares.
2. **Legal wrapper** — an SPV, fund or trust legally owns the asset and issues claims on it. This is what gives the token holder rights.
3. **Custodian** — a bank or qualified custodian holds the asset (e.g. BNY for many Treasury funds).
4. **Issuer + transfer agent** — mints tokens 1:1 against the asset and keeps the official record (Securitize does this for BlackRock's BUIDL).
5. **Blockchain** — tokens live on Ethereum, Solana, Avalanche, etc. Most are *permissioned*: only KYC-approved (whitelisted) wallets can hold them.
6. **Oracles + attestations** — feed prices/NAV on-chain and prove the reserves exist (Chainlink, RedStone, auditor reports).

Two flavors matter a lot:

- **Issuer-sponsored / custodial** — the token *is* (or is backed 1:1 by) the real security. You have real ownership rights.
- **Synthetic** — the token only tracks the price. You own a contract with the issuer, not the asset. The SEC draws a hard line between these.

**Regulation, quick timeline**

- Jan 28, 2026: SEC staff statement — a tokenized security is still a security; splits issuer-sponsored vs third-party (custodial or synthetic).
- Mar–Apr 2026: Nasdaq and NYSE approved to trade tokenized shares on the same order book as regular shares.
- Sept 17, 2026: SEC five-year Innovation Exemption for trading tokenized stocks on-chain — but it excludes synthetic tokens like xStocks ([Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/winners-and-losers-of-the-secs-new-tokenized-stocks-rules)).

## Why people care

- **24/7 trading and near-instant settlement** — no waiting for market hours or T+1.
- **Yield on-chain** — crypto holders can park cash in T-bill tokens earning ~3.4–3.5% instead of a 0% stablecoin.
- **Fractional access** — own a slice of assets that used to need big minimums.
- **Collateral** — tokens like BUIDL can be posted as collateral on exchanges and DeFi lending (Aave, Uniswap listings).
- **Cheaper back office** — fewer middlemen reconciling records.

## Risks and red flags

- **Off-chain trust** — the token is only as good as the custodian, issuer and legal wrapper. Code can't verify the gold is in the vault.
- **Synthetic vs real** — many tokenized stocks give price exposure only: no voting, maybe no dividends, and you're exposed to the issuer going bust.
- **Liquidity is thinner than advertised** — 24/7 trading doesn't mean there's a buyer at 3am; redemptions may only happen on business days.
- **Access limits** — the biggest products are restricted: BUIDL needs $5M and qualified-purchaser status; USDY is non-US only.
- **Regulation still moving** — the SEC exemption is five years and narrow; rules can change.
- **Smart-contract and oracle risk** — bugs or bad price feeds can break things.
- **Real estate pitches** — under 1% of the market; be skeptical of anyone leading with it.
- **Hype tokens** — a governance token (like ONDO) is not the same as owning the underlying T-bills.

## Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| RWA | Real-world asset represented by a token on a blockchain |
| Tokenization | Recording ownership of an asset as a blockchain token |
| NAV | Net asset value — what each fund share is worth |
| SPV | Special purpose vehicle — a legal entity that holds the asset |
| Custodian | The regulated firm that actually holds the asset |
| Oracle | Service that feeds off-chain data (prices, reserves) onto the blockchain |

| Synthetic | Token tracks a price but doesn't give ownership of the asset |
| DeFi | Decentralized finance — lending and trading via smart contracts |
